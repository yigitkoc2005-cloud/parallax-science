Question: Why Doesn't Freezing Kill This Frog?

You see ice outside a frog's cells. So why doesn't the freeze kill it? As ice grows between cells, water shifts outward. Pressure can damage shrinking cells. The liver senses ice and breaks stored glycogen into glucose, then releases that sugar into the blood. Glucose helps protect tissues as water shifts, cushioning cells and limiting some damage. But here's the strange part: the heart can stop and breathing pause while much of the frog freezes. Yet most cells do not become solid ice. Ice forms mainly outside them, and glucose helps reduce harm from the squeeze. When warmth returns, ice melts. The heart may beat again, and the frog may move. How organs manage repeated freeze-thaw cycles remains under study. Ice surrounds the cells, then melts; the frog can return to motion.
